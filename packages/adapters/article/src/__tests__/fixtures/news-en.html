Why cities are planting tiny forests

Share on Facebook Share on X

Across Europe, city councils are turning neglected corners of land into dense patches of native trees, a technique that promises a fully grown woodland in a fraction of the usual time.

The method, first developed by a Japanese botanist in the 1970s, involves planting saplings of dozens of species just a few centimetres apart, which forces them to compete for light and shoot upwards.

Advertisement

Supporters say these miniature forests cool the streets around them, soak up heavy rain and give residents a reason to look after a shared space, though critics warn that the benefits are often overstated.

"People were sceptical at first," said one organiser, who has helped plant more than twenty sites. "Then they saw a bare car park become a thicket in three summers, and the mood changed completely."

Researchers caution that a handful of small plots cannot make up for the loss of old woodland, but they agree that every extra tree in a dense neighbourhood makes a measurable difference on a hot afternoon.
